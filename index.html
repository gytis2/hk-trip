Three things this plan is built around

The rest is routing. These are the decisions everything else bends to.

Wednesday 14 October is a Happy Valley race night. Night racing at Happy Valley only runs on Wednesdays and the October fixture list confirms the 14th. HK$10 to get in, free for tourists over 18 with a passport. It's the best night in the city and it dictates that Wednesday is your Hong Kong Island day — the racecourse, the Monster Building and the sunset summit are all on the same MTR line.
Saturday 17 October is the start of a 3-day public holiday weekend. Chung Yeung Festival is Sunday the 18th, with Monday the 19th off. So the last day stays in Kowloon on the doorstep — housing estates, temples and a food crawl, all within twenty minutes of your bags. No islands, no hikes, no Peak Tram queue on that day.
You're flying in from Malaysia, so there's no jet lag. Kuala Lumpur and Hong Kong are both UTC+8. That's why this plan starts at 07:00 twice without apology — you'll wake up fine. Sunset is 18:00 all week, sunrise 06:18, which is what puts the two summit stops where they are.

Tian Tan Buddha & Po Lin Monastery

268 steps up to a 34-metre bronze Buddha sitting on a mountain, with nothing but green ridgelines behind it. At this hour there will be incense smoke, monks, and almost no one else. Walk the ring of six bronze devas around the base, then come back down for Po Lin Monastery — scarlet and gold, enormous incense coils, and the smell of sandalwood over everything.

Then the Wisdom Path, ten minutes further on: 38 timber columns carved with the Heart Sutra, standing in a figure-of-eight on an open hillside. Usually empty. It's the better photograph of the two.

Best frame: from the bottom of the stairs, low and centred, so the Buddha crowns the steps. Then from the platform looking back down over the monastery roofs.

Tai O fishing village

What it actually is: a fishing settlement on the far western tip of Lantau, built by the Tanka boat people — a community who historically lived on the water and were barred from owning land. Their houses are wooden shacks up on stilts, standing directly in a tidal creek, lashed together with walkways and linked by a footbridge. A couple of thousand people still live there. The whole place smells of dried shrimp paste, which is what it has made and sold for centuries.

It is not a museum and not a resort — it's a working, visibly poor village that happens to look like nowhere else in Hong Kong. That's exactly why it photographs the way it does, and also why some visitors come away uneasy about having wandered through it. Worth knowing before you commit: if that sits badly with you, skip it and take the cable car down early instead. If you do go, buy something — the dried seafood stalls and the sampan operators are the village's actual income.

Take the HK$30 sampan ride — twenty minutes out through the stilt houses and into the open sea to look for pink dolphins. Eat as you walk: grilled squid on a stick, giant fish balls, egg waffles, and the shrimp paste everything here smells of. Then climb ten minutes up to the Tai O Heritage Hotel, a whitewashed colonial police station on the headland, for the view back over the roofs.

Best frame: the footbridge at the centre of the village looking down the creek — stilt houses both sides, boats below, mountains behind.

Yum cha — proper morning dim sum

London Restaurant, 612 Nathan Road, Mong Kok — about 15 minutes' walk north. A big old-school dim sum hall that still does it the vanishing way: carpet, round tables, and ladies pushing steel trolleys between them. You flag a cart, point, and they stamp your card. Hong Kong has only about ten of these left.

Go early, when it's all retirees with newspapers. Get har gow, siu mai, char siu bao, cheung fun and an egg tart. Tap two fingers on the table when someone pours your tea. Bring cash. You can take the MTR to Tsim Sha Tsui from Mong Kok station right afterwards.

Backup if it's closed: Kam Kong Restaurant, 56 Dundas Street, Mong Kok — also trolleys, opens 07:00.

Lugard Road loop — the postcard view

This is the shot everyone knows, and you don't need a ticket for it. Man Mo Temple & Hollywood Road

Walk west along Hollywood Road past the antique shops and Cat Street to Man Mo Temple (1847). Tai Kwun + PMQ

Tai Kwun is the old Central Police Station, magistracy and Victoria Prison, restored into a free arts complex — granite courtyards, colonial verandahs, and the JC Contemporary spiral staircase, a perfect aluminium helix that is one of the most photographed interiors in the city. Ding Ding tram, Central → Quarry Bay

Running since 1904, and the only all-double-decker tram fleet left in the world. Three Hong Kong dollars flat, however far you go. Climb to the upper deck, take the very front seat, and ride east through Wan Chai, Causeway Bay and North Point with the windows open and the city passing at walking pace an arm's length away.

The trade: this replaces the 20-minute MTR hop to Quarry Bay and takes about 50, so it costs you half an hour on the tightest day of the trip. Worth it for the ride — but if you're already running late after Tai Kwun, take the train and save the tram for another afternoon. You tap in at the back and pay at the front when you get off.

The Monster Building, Quarry Bay

Five 1960s housing blocks — Yick Cheong, Yick Fat, Montane Mansion and neighbours — built into a single E-shaped mass around a courtyard, about 2,200 flats of pastel concrete and air-conditioning units stacked twenty storeys straight up with no sky visible. It was in Transformers and Ghost in the Shell, and in person it is genuinely overwhelming.

Be decent about it. Real people live here and there's a banner asking visitors to seek permission before photographing. Go in quietly, shoot from the courtyard, stay ten minutes, don't use a tripod, don't shout, leave. That's the deal that keeps it open.

Best frame: stand in the middle of the courtyard, phone flat on your palm pointing straight up, ultra-wide. Then get very low against the far wall for the vertical.

Red Incense Burner Summit — sunset at 18:00

From the Braemar Hill bus terminus it's a fifteen-minute climb, half a kilometre, easy, up to a bare rock outcrop that looks straight down the throat of Victoria Harbour with Kowloon opposite and the whole of North Point below. Locals rate it above the Peak, and on the evidence they're right — you're lower, closer, and the harbour fills the frame instead of sitting on the horizon.

Get there by 17:40 to claim a spot on the rock. Sunset is 18:00, and the real picture is at 18:10–18:30 when the city lights come up and the sky is still deep blue.

Bring a phone torch. The descent after 18:40 is genuinely pitch black and the trail has loose rock.

Happy Valley night racing

A floodlit turf track sitting in a bowl of apartment towers, eight races between roughly 19:00 and 23:00, and a crowd of bankers, retirees, tourists and very serious punters all drinking in a beer garden beside the rail. Entry is HK$10 tapped in with your Octopus at Entrance G — and tourists over 18 with a passport get the public enclosure free.

Minimum bet is HK$10. Put a few dollars on each race, stand at the rail for the final furlong, eat a sausage from the stand. Arriving by taxi at this time you'll catch almost the whole card. No singlets or flip-flops.

Best frame: from the rail at the finish line, slow shutter as they come past, with the tower blocks lit behind the far turn.

Taipa Village, Rua do Cunha & the Taipa Houses

Three blocks of pedestrian food street backed by lanes of pastel colonial shophouses. Graze rather than sit: a pork chop bun at Sei Kee Cafe (a fried chop in a crusty roll, nothing else, and it's perfect), curry fish balls, serradura, and free samples of almond cookie and pork jerky pushed at you from every bakery counter on the street.

Then walk ten minutes to the Taipa Houses — five mint-green Portuguese villas on stilts above the wetland, with a tiled promenade and the Cotai skyline rising absurdly behind them. This is the best photograph in Macau after St Paul's.

Best frame: the Taipa Houses from the far end of the promenade, so the row of green villas leads the eye into the casino towers. The contrast is the picture.

Coloane: Lord Stow's, and the yellow chapel

A sleepy waterfront village of yellow and white buildings that feels like a Portuguese fishing town that drifted east. Lord Stow's Bakery is here — the original shop, where an English pharmacist invented the Macanese version of the Portuguese egg tart in 1989: MOP 12, caramelised almost black on top, still warm.

On the Michelin question: Lord Stow's is listed in the MICHELIN Guide for Macau, but it has no star and no Bib Gourmand — it's a bakery counter, not a restaurant. So: a genuinely famous tart, properly good, about €1.30. Not a pilgrimage. Margaret's Café e Nata on the Peninsula makes an arguably better one and is five minutes from your hotel on Friday morning. If the tart is the only reason you'd come out to Coloane, do Margaret's instead and skip this stop — the village is the real argument for the trip.

Then the butter-yellow Chapel of St Francis Xavier on its little square, the library with green shutters, and the waterfront looking across to mainland China. If you want lunch proper, Restaurante Fernando near Hac Sa beach is the Portuguese institution.

Best frame: the chapel square straight on, symmetrical, with the palm trees either side.

Your budget

Option B: Casinos — actually play

Macau makes more from gambling than Las Vegas, and it's baccarat country — most tables are baccarat, which is also the simplest game in the building: bet on Player or Banker and the dealer does the rest. Sic bo (three dice) is the other easy one to try.

Where:

Casino Lisboa / Grand Lisboa, Peninsula — about 10 minutes by taxi from your hotel. The old-school one, the lowest table minimums, and the most local crowd. Grand Lisboa's golden lotus tower next door is the most Macau building there is.

The Venetian, Cotai — reachable by free shuttle, the biggest floor with the widest range of stakes, and the canals upstairs if you want a break.

What it costs to sit down: live baccarat on the main floors starts around HK$100–500 (≈ €11–55) a hand on the Peninsula and HK$500–1,000+ (≈ €55–110) in Cotai. The cheap way in is the electronic and "stadium" baccarat — real dealers on big screens, bets from about HK$20–50 (≈ €2–5). Chips are in Hong Kong dollars; buy them at the table or the cashier cage.

The rules: you must be 21+ and carry your passport — it's checked at the door. No photos anywhere on the gaming floor. Smart casual; no one will stop you in a T-shirt, but avoid flip-flops and gym shorts. Unlike Vegas, drinks aren't free, and the main floors are smoke-free.

Two tips: decide your budget before you walk in, bring exactly that in cash, and leave the cards at the hotel. And ignore anyone friendly who offers to show you how to play or lend you chips — only ever take chips from the dealer or the cage.

The one rule that matters. Choi Hung, Ping Shek, Lok Wah and the Monster Building are people's homes, not sets. There are already signs at Quarry Bay asking visitors to seek permission, and photography inside Lai Tak Tsuen is banned outright because of how tourists behaved there. Go early, keep your voice down, no tripods, no drones, don't block a doorway, and be gone in ten minutes. These spots stay open because most visitors behave.

Swaps, if the weather or the mood turns

Four substitutions that don't break the routing.

Rain on Tuesday → do Wednesday's plan instead

Lantau in cloud is a wasted HK$235 — the Buddha disappears and the cable car shows you fog. Swap the two days entirely; Central, Man Mo, Tai Kwun and the Monster Building all work wet. Just remember the races are Wednesday only, so if you swap, do the Island day without them and take the Peak on Wednesday morning instead.

You'd rather hike than shoot estates

Replace Saturday's Kowloon circuit with Dragon's Back — 8 km along a sea ridge to Big Wave Bay, 2–3 hours, beginner level. But it's a holiday Saturday, so start by 08:00 and accept company on the trail. You'd lose Choi Hung, which is the better photograph.

You want an island day instead of Lantau

Sai Kung plus a sampan to Sharp Island — the volcanic sandbar you can walk across at low tide — and seafood picked from the tanks on the promenade. More relaxing than Tuesday, less spectacular, and about the same money.

Typhoon signal goes up

At signal 8 the city shuts: MTR limited, ferries stopped, most shops closed. Mid-October it's unlikely but possible. Indoor fallbacks within reach of base: M+ and the Hong Kong Palace Museum at West Kowloon, 25 minutes on foot from Shanghai Street, plus Langham Place in Mong Kok. Check hko.gov.hk each morning.
